Game-playing AI · Technical breakdown

Solving PolyTrack // teaching a car to drive a full lap — by searching the real physics, not by learning

A racing game. A car that has to complete a lap. And an AI that, after reinforcement learning flatly refused to work, plans the lap move-by-move inside a bit-exact copy of the game's own physics. Here's the whole machine — and the bug hunt that made it drive.

CH.01

The task, stated plainly

PolyTrack is a low-poly time-trial racer: you hold four keys — accelerate, brake, steer left, steer right — and thread a car through checkpoints to a finish line, as fast as possible. Simple to describe, brutal to automate.

The car obeys real driving physics — momentum, grip, suspension, airtime. The tracks fold over themselves with ramps, jumps and stacked decks. A human learns the racing line in a few laps. A program has to discover, out of billions of possible key-press sequences, the one narrow ribbon of inputs that reaches the finish without spinning out or flying off the track.

CH.02

First attempt: let it learn. It didn't.

The obvious move is reinforcement learning — let the car try, reward progress, evolve a neural-net driver. We ran a parallel Evolution Strategies trainer for days. It never finished a single lap.

The stall. ~600 generations, 33 hours of compute, best reward frozen at 2768, and zero of 24 cars ever crossing the line. The population that dared to accelerate mostly crashed — so evolution learned to creep, not to race.

The reason is structural. The reward is sparse — you learn almost nothing until you finish, and you can't finish until you've already learned a great deal. The search space is enormous, the physics unforgiving, and a policy that's 99% right still flies off the same corner every time. RL wasn't converging on this track; it was drifting.

Verdict

Learning a driver from scratch was the wrong tool. We stopped trying to learn the lap and started trying to solve it.

CH.03

The pivot: search the real physics

Instead of training a network, plan directly. Run a tree search that tries key-presses inside an exact clone of the game engine, keeps the branches that make progress, and commits to them a fraction of a second at a time. Three parts make it work.

The pipeline. The engine says what happens; the field says where to go; the search decides how. Because the engine is deterministic, the search can save a state, explore thousands of futures, and rewind to reality exactly.

Why determinism is the secret ingredient: the physics reimplementation is bit-for-bit identical to the game (it passes a self-test), so a saved snapshot replays identically every time. That's what makes tree search possible at all — you can branch the future, measure it, and undo it without drift.

CH.04

The guidance field: which way is forward?

A search needs a compass. The naive one — straight-line distance to the next checkpoint — is a trap: on a winding track it points the car straight through walls. So we build a better one.

Euclidean vs. geodesic. We voxelise the drivable road into ~8,800 cells and run Dijkstra outward from each checkpoint, so every cell knows the true metres of track left to drive (colour = cost-to-go). The gradient always points along the road — never through a barrier.
Potential

Cost-to-go

Metres of road remaining to the finish. Lower is better — the search is rewarded for shrinking it.

Route target

Where to aim

A point on the road a few metres ahead, found by rolling downhill on the field. The steering aims here.

Speed cap

How fast is safe

A per-cell speed limit derived from the road's curvature — and, later, its steepness. This detail becomes the hero of the story.

CH.05

Windowed MCTS: search a little, commit a little

You can't tree-search a whole 25-second lap at once — the branches explode. So the solver works in a sliding window: search the next slice of the future in depth, lock only the part it's most sure of, slide forward, and search again.

Anatomy of one window. Inputs are chunked into 20 ms blocks (16 possible key combinations each). Thousands of simulations grow a UCT tree over the window and roll out to a horizon, scoring each by geodesic progress, exit speed, airtime, slip and overspeed. The most-visited chain gets locked; the window slides; repeat until the finish.
Confidence gate + rewind

Lock only what's sure — and back up when stuck

A move is committed only if enough simulations agree. When progress stalls (a hard corner or a jump), the solver rewinds a second or two, reseeds its randomness, and attacks the spot again — up to 40 fresh attempts.

Root-parallel consensus

A dozen searchers voting at once

Twelve worker threads search the same window with different random seeds, then merge their visit counts into one consensus before locking. Because the engine is deterministic, they all advance in perfect lockstep.

A speed trick

The guidance field also powers a fast rollout driver — a reflex steering policy that just follows the route target and respects the speed cap. It drives the boring opening of the lap instantly, so the expensive tree search only spends its effort where the track is actually hard. We call it seeding.

CH.06

The bug hunt (this is the good part)

The architecture was sound and the car still couldn't finish. Every run died at the same corner of Summer 1. Fixing it meant becoming a forensic investigator of a physics engine — and it's where the real story lives.

Bug #1 — The car was flying off a cliff

After the first checkpoint, the road plunges from a height of ~57 down to ~3. Tracing the car frame-by-frame, we caught it: it hit the drop at 130 km/h, went fully airborne — wheels off the ground the entire descent — sailed past the landing, and dropped onto a dead-end lower ledge where it was trapped forever. The guidance field had put no speed limit on the descent, because it only measured horizontal curvature, and a straight ramp looks perfectly gentle from above.

The fix: elevation-aware speed caps. We taught the guidance field to read the gradient of the road ahead, not just its curvature, and cap the approach speed on steep descents. The car now stays glued to the ramp, lands clean, and rockets out the bottom at 240 km/h. This one change unblocked what every prior run — RL included — had died on for months.

Bug #2 — A wall the map couldn't see

Past the jump, the car floors it down a straight and then, at a specific spot, slams from 251 km/h to 43 in a single frame and stops dead. Replaying the exact trajectory showed a physical wall where the low surface steps up to the main road. The catch: the guidance field is built only from drivable road faces — vertical walls are filtered out — so the map cheerfully routes the car straight through a wall it doesn't know exists.

The trace that named the bug. A vertical wall is invisible to a road-only map. This is an honest, still-open limit — not a tuning knob.
CH.07

Where it stands — and what it took

The solver now drives further into the track than anything before it — clearing the launch, the checkpoint, and the jump that had stopped every attempt for months. A full finishing lap is close, but not yet in hand.

The honest headline

Reinforcement learning couldn't finish a lap in 33 hours. Search over the real physics — deterministic engine, geodesic map, windowed MCTS — drives the hard majority of the track and turned an impossible problem into a debuggable one.

REF

Terms, for the narration

Quick, say-out-loud definitions you can drop into the video.

Deterministic sim
A copy of the game's physics that gives the exact same result every time — so you can save the moment, try a thousand futures, and rewind.
Geodesic field
A map where every spot on the road knows the real driving distance left to the finish. Its downhill direction is "forward."
Speed cap
A per-spot speed limit from the road's shape — sharpness of the corner and, crucially, steepness of the drop.
MCTS
Monte-Carlo Tree Search: try lots of move sequences, keep exploring the promising ones, commit to the winner.
Window / lock / slide
Search a short slice of the future, commit only the part you're sure of, move forward, repeat.
Rollout
A fast, rough drive to the horizon used to score how good a branch looks without searching it in full.
Seeding
Letting a simple reflex driver handle the easy opening so the expensive search only works the hard parts.
Rewind
When the car gets stuck, back up a couple of seconds, shuffle the randomness, and try the spot again.
